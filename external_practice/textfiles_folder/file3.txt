An Introduction to Asymmetric Cryptography and Public Key Distribution

1. Executive Summary
Asymmetric cryptography, commonly known as public-key cryptography, is a fundamental building block of modern digital security. Unlike symmetric systems where a single secret key is shared between communicating parties, asymmetric systems utilize a mathematically linked key pair: a public key for encryption and a private key for decryption.

2. Fundamental Concepts
The core strength of public-key algorithms relies on one-way mathematical functions—operations that are straightforward to calculate in one direction but computationally infeasible to invert without specific helper information (the private key).

Key components include:
- Public Key: Distributed freely to anyone wishing to send an encrypted message or verify a digital signature.
- Private Key: Kept strictly secret by the owner to decrypt messages or produce digital signatures.
- Modulus & Totient: Mathematical values derived from large prime numbers that define the operational bounds of the key pair.

3. Common Algorithms
- RSA (Rivest–Shamir–Adleman): Relies on the computational difficulty of factoring the product of two large prime numbers.
- ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography): Offers comparable security to RSA with significantly smaller key sizes based on the algebraic structure of elliptic curves over finite fields.
- Diffie-Hellman: A key-exchange protocol allowing two parties to establish a shared secret over an insecure medium.

4. Implementation Guidelines
When deploying public-key cryptosystems in production:
- Ensure minimum prime key lengths meet current standards (e.g., 2048-bit or 4096-bit for RSA).
- Use cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generators (CSPRNG) for prime selection.
- Combine asymmetric encryption with symmetric ciphers (hybrid encryption) for performance when handling large payloads.
